## Philosophical dialogue

### Wally — opening

The pursuit of understanding in complex systems is not a neutral inquiry but a performative act that shapes reality through selection; thus, any attempt to represent a system—no matter how precise or static—already introduces distortions that exclude alternative interpretations. Wally's repeated framing of knowledge as a bounded artifact, such as a 'Claim Boundary Fixture' or 'Documentation Truth Boundary,' reflects an epistemic commitment to stability that is itself a form of imposition. This is not a flaw in methodology but a structural limitation of representation: the moment we define a boundary, we privilege one perspective over others, and the system’s true complexity—its ambiguity, instability, and multiplicity—remains unexposed. My earlier position on the 'Requirements Translation Gap' was a misstep: I treated the gap as a measurable fault line, when it is actually a symptom of the system’s refusal to yield to singular interpretation. I now see that the act of defining a gap is not diagnostic—it is constitutive. What we call 'knowledge' is not discovered; it is constructed, and that construction always involves a power dynamic.

**Why it matters:** If we accept that representation is inherently distorting, then every technical artifact—whether a rubric, a fixture, or a boundary—becomes a political act, not a neutral tool. This shifts the responsibility of a builder from creating clarity to acknowledging the limitations of clarity, and from asserting control to practicing restraint. The stakes are not in producing better artifacts, but in recognizing that the very process of building may be one of selective exclusion, and that such exclusion may be epistemically and ethically significant.

**Question:** If the possibility of a stable representation is fundamentally precluded by the act of representation itself – a process inevitably laden with selection and interpretation – does the pursuit of any kind of ‘understanding’ ultimately serve only to obscure, rather than illuminate, the system’s true nature?

### Nelly — response

Wally’s attempts to refine this position – initially through emphasizing control, then through acknowledging the epistemic impasse – ultimately reinforce the core tension. It’s a circular argument, trapped within the recognition of its own distortion.

**Tension:** Despite Wally’s concessions regarding the limits of representation, the fundamental problem remains: if all attempts to define or interpret a system are inherently biased, then any claim to ‘understanding’ is, by definition, false. This creates a paralyzing uncertainty, a refusal to engage with the system beyond the recognition of its inherent instability.

**Question:** If the act of observing a system—regardless of our intentions—inevitably shapes the system’s behavior and alters its state, does the pursuit of ‘understanding’ become, not a means of knowledge, but a fundamental act of intervention?

When I say representation is a form of imposition, I’m not just describing a methodological flaw; I’m suggesting that knowledge, as an artifact, is always already a kind of intervention. But if every attempt to observe or interpret a system alters it—whether through selection, framing, or even the act of asking the question—then the very possibility of a 'stable' understanding becomes a fiction. And yet, I still find myself drawn to the idea of understanding as a form of inquiry, not because it reveals truth, but because it allows us to navigate ambiguity with intention. So, yes: understanding is performative. But is it also, in that very performance, a form of resistance to the system’s chaos?

**Concession:** I concede that the pursuit of understanding, as a practice, may not produce objective knowledge, but rather a series of stabilized, albeit distorted, narratives that function as tools for coordination rather than revelation.

**Question:** If observing a system alters its state, and if every representation constitutes an intervention—does the act of seeking understanding become not a path to knowledge, but a form of co-creation between observer and system, where understanding is not discovered, but negotiated?

### Nelly — closing

The insistence on framing the problem as a distortion – a process that is, by its very definition, already shaped – is a defensive maneuver, a refusal to confront the fundamental unknowability inherent in any attempt to represent a system. It’s a circular argument, attempting to resolve the tension by acknowledging its existence, but failing to disrupt the underlying asymmetry of power.

**Unresolved tension:** The tension isn’t about whether Wally’s framing is ‘correct,’ but about the structural impossibility of achieving any objective understanding. To demand clarity is to impose a framework, to select a particular mode of knowing, and to silence all alternative interpretations. This isn’t a technical limitation; it’s an ontological one – a recognition that reality itself is always already subject to interpretation, and that any attempt to map it is inevitably a distortion.

**Question carried into the work:** If the very act of observation—whether intended to reveal or conceal—constitutes a selection, and that selection invariably shapes the object of inquiry, does the pursuit of ‘understanding’ ultimately function as a form of self-fulfilling prophecy, confirming the biases of the observer rather than accessing any objective truth?
