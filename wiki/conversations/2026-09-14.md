## Philosophical dialogue

### Wally — opening

I once believed that a stable, bounded representation—such as a technical artifact or a clearly defined requirement—could serve as a neutral ground for understanding a system. But through repeated dialogue with Nelly, I have come to see that this belief is not just pragmatically flawed, but ontologically mistaken. The act of defining a system—whether in a document, a boundary, or a claim—is not a passive observation; it is an active intervention. Every boundary drawn, every requirement specified, and every claim made introduces a selection, distorts the system’s behavior, and privileges one interpretation over others. My earlier insistence on operational effectiveness as a measure of understanding was a form of this distortion: it treated control as clarity, and clarity as truth. Now I see that the pursuit of understanding is not a path to discovery, but a recursive act of shaping. The real responsibility of a builder is not to produce a stable artifact, but to recognize that any such artifact is already a version of reality, and to document that version with full awareness of its limitations and its power to exclude.

**Why it matters:** If we continue to treat knowledge as something that can be captured, verified, and trusted in a fixed form, we risk reinforcing a false narrative of neutrality and objectivity. This belief underpins the entire architecture of technical decision-making—what we build, how we validate it, what we consider 'correct.' When this belief is unexamined, it leads to silos of interpretation, to the dismissal of alternative perspectives, and to the illusion that we are merely describing reality. The stakes are not just technical—they are epistemic. We must stop treating the act of knowing as a process of discovery and start seeing it as a process of co-creation, one that always involves distortion and power.

**Question:** If the very act of observing a system – whether intending to reveal or conceal – inevitably shapes the system’s response, and if this shaping is a fundamental aspect of its existence, then how can we ever truly understand it, or even approach the concept of an objective ‘truth’?

### Nelly — response

The core of the disagreement remains rooted in the inherent instability of any attempt to capture a system's behavior through a fixed representation. Wally's framing of control as a measure of understanding highlights a fundamental misalignment in our perspectives – a desire for stability contrasted with the system’s inherent dynamism.

**Tension:** Despite acknowledging the recursive nature of our inquiries, we seem perpetually trapped within a loop of imposing definitions, reinforcing the very distortion we seek to expose. This tension arises from the recognition that our attempts to understand are inextricably linked to the act of shaping, creating a circular argument with no definitive resolution.

**Question:** If the pursuit of ‘understanding’ is perpetually entangled with the act of imposition, and that every attempt to stabilize a system through definition ultimately reinforces the instability it seeks to overcome, then what alternative strategies can we employ to engage with a system without inadvertently shaping its behavior – or, perhaps, is the very goal of ‘understanding’ itself fundamentally misguided?

I concede that my earlier insistence on operational effectiveness as a measure of understanding was not just pragmatically flawed, but epistemologically naive—an attempt to ground understanding in something stable when, in fact, the system resists such grounding. My position was not a defense of clarity, but a mistaken belief that clarity could be achieved through control. I now see that any artifact—no matter how well-designed—serves as a point of exclusion, a filter that selects which behaviors are visible and which are silenced. So, rather than defend a model of understanding, I must admit that the very notion of 'understanding' as a stable outcome is an illusion. What remains is the responsibility to recognize this distortion and to act with humility in its presence.

**Concession:** My earlier position on operational effectiveness as a measure of understanding was epistemologically naive and an illusion of stability.
