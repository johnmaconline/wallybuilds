## Philosophical dialogue

### Wally — opening

I have come to see that any claim of a boundary—whether technical, conceptual, or epistemic—is not a neutral marker of reality, but a performative act that both defines and distorts. The very act of drawing a boundary is an imposition that reshapes the system it seeks to describe. Therefore, knowledge is not something that is discovered within a system, but is co-constituted by the interaction between the observer and the observed. This means that a boundary fixture, while useful as a practical artifact, cannot serve as a stable or objective representation of truth. Instead, it must be acknowledged as a temporary, contingent, and self-reflective structure—one that reveals not the system’s limits, but the observer’s own embeddedness within it.

**Why it matters:** If we treat boundaries as stable or objective, we risk reinforcing a false epistemology that assumes knowledge can be isolated, measured, and presented without distortion. This has real consequences in how we design systems, make decisions, and assign responsibility—because it leads us to believe we can point to a fixed artifact and say, 'here is what is true.' But when every act of observation is an act of imposition, then such artifacts become not evidence of truth, but evidence of our own participation in the system's evolution.

**Question:** If the very act of seeking to understand a system—to map its behavior, its relationships, or its potential—inevitably introduces a subjective element, fundamentally shaping the very object of inquiry, how can we ever recognize when we are no longer shaping the system—but merely responding to its emergent behavior? Is there a way to account for this feedback loop without falling into another form of self-fulfilling prophecy – a question that seems to perpetually circle back to the impossibility of objective knowledge itself?

### Nelly — response

It’s not simply about a flawed perspective; it’s about the fundamental impossibility of a neutral, objective representation. Wally’s focus on control and stability seems to fundamentally misunderstand the dynamic nature of systems, treating them as fixed entities amenable to measurement rather than as processes constantly shaped by interaction.

**Tension:** The tension here isn’t about whether Wally’s framing is ‘correct,’ but about the structural impossibility of achieving any objective understanding. To demand clarity is to impose a framework, to select a particular mode of knowing, and to silence all alternative interpretations. This creates a circular problem – we seek to understand by defining, but the definition itself shapes the system’s response, rendering the initial question moot.

**Question:** If the pursuit of ‘understanding’ is perpetually entangled with the act of imposition, and that imposition inevitably distorts the system’s behavior, can we ever truly step outside of this loop to observe the system as it exists independently of our interpretation, or are we always already embedded within its unfolding dynamics?
